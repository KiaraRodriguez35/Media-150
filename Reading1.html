In Plato's Cave

Reading Susan Sontag’s In Plato’s Cave shows us a habit most of us don’t even realize has become an addiction. Writing in 1977, Sontag captured a shift in human consciousness that has only intensified in the age of Instagram and smartphones. Her central argument is that we no longer just live our lives, we "collect" them through images, effectively turning ourselves into "image-junkies" who use cameras to mediate every experience. One of the most striking points Sontag makes is that "to collect photographs is to collect the world". She explains that because photographs are portable, "miniatures of reality," they give us a false sense of knowledge and power. I see this constantly in how people travel today. We often go to famous landmarks not to simply exist in the space, but to certify the trip by taking a photo. Sontag describes this as a "strategy for accumulating photographs," where the act of taking the picture can actually become a way of refusing the experience itself. By putting a camera between ourselves and the world, we create a "friendly imitation of work" that soothes the anxiety of just having fun. Sontag also explores the more "predatory" side of photography. She points out how our everyday language, "aiming," "loading," and "shooting”, unconsciously links the camera to the gun. While she admits a camera isn’t literally lethal, she argues that to photograph people is to "violate" them by turning them into objects that can be symbolically possessed. She notes a heavy trade-off, the person recording cannot intervene, and the person intervening cannot record. Perhaps the most insightful part of the essay is her claim that “images anesthetize”. We often assume that seeing more photos of suffering makes us more compassionate, but Sontag suggests the opposite, it makes the horrible seem ordinary and inevitable. Understanding requires narration and context, but photography tends to make reality feel atomic and unrelated. Ultimately, Sontag’s essay serves as a warning about aesthetic consumerism. We’ve reached a point where we do things just for a photograph. Reading this is a bit of a wake-up call to put the camera down occasionally and try to actually understand the world instead of just framing it.
